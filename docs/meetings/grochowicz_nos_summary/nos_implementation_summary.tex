\begin{abstract}
\noindent
GreenBubble is a PyPSA-based capacity-expansion model of an industrial
Power-to-X hub (electrolysis, methanolisation, biogas upgrading, storage,
symbiosis). This note summarises how we implemented \emph{Modelling to
Generate Alternatives} (MGA) / near-optimal space exploration on top of it,
following Neumann \& Brown (2021) for the near-optimal space itself and
Grochowicz et al.\ (2023) for the multi-year robustness extension. It is
written for a working discussion: Section~\ref{sec:provenance} is
deliberately explicit about \emph{exactly} what we reused from PyPSA's own
MGA machinery versus what we implemented from scratch, since we did not have
access to a Grochowicz-authored reference implementation and would value
comparing notes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
% =====================================================================
A cost-optimal capacity-expansion solve returns exactly one design. In
practice, many \emph{other} designs are within a few percent of that cost
and can differ substantially in technology mix. Exploring this
\emph{near-optimal feasible space} answers questions the single optimum
cannot: which technologies are load-bearing versus fully substitutable, how
much room exists to steer the design toward secondary goals, and whether one
design stays good across several weather/price years rather than being
over-fitted to the one year it was solved for.

GreenBubble needed this for an additional reason beyond the usual
single-country capacity-expansion setting: it is optimised in two distinct
\emph{modes} --- \texttt{demand} mode (minimise cost to meet fixed product
demands, objective $\geq 0$) and \texttt{price} mode (maximise profit
selling at fixed prices, which shows up in the LP as \emph{minimising} a
cost objective that is typically \emph{negative}, since revenue exceeds
cost). Any near-optimal formulation had to be correct in both regimes, and
also compose with GreenBubble's stochastic (multi-scenario) optimisation
mode. None of this changes the underlying mathematics of Neumann \& Brown or
Grochowicz et al.\ --- it just meant we could not use the reference
formulation completely as-is and had to generalise the sign convention (see
Section~\ref{sec:budget}).

\section{Mathematical background}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The cost-optimal LP and the $\varepsilon$-near-optimal space}
Write the capacity-expansion model as the linear program
\begin{equation}
  \min_{x}\; c^\top x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad Ax \le b,
\end{equation}
where $x$ collects every decision variable (investment \emph{and}
operation), $c^\top x$ is total system cost, and $c_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is its
optimal value. Following Neumann \& Brown (2021) and Grochowicz et al.\
(2023), the \textbf{$\varepsilon$-near-optimal feasible space} is the slice
of the feasible region whose cost is within a relative slack $\varepsilon$
of the optimum:
\begin{equation}
  X_\varepsilon \;=\; \bigl\{\, x : Ax \le b,\;\; c^\top x \le
  (1+\varepsilon)\, c_{\mathrm{opt}} \,\bigr\}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sign-robust budget for price-driven (negative-objective) runs}
\label{sec:budget}
GreenBubble adds exactly this extra constraint to the model (the
\emph{budget}), but in a sign-robust form so it also holds when
$c_{\mathrm{opt}} < 0$ (price mode, net revenue):
\begin{equation}
  c^\top x \;\le\; c_{\mathrm{opt}} + \varepsilon\,\lvert c_{\mathrm{opt}}
  \rvert
  \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    (1+\varepsilon)\, c_{\mathrm{opt}}, & c_{\mathrm{opt}} \ge 0
      \quad \text{(demand mode)} \\[2pt]
    (1-\varepsilon)\, c_{\mathrm{opt}}, & c_{\mathrm{opt}} < 0
      \quad \text{(price mode)}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Both branches keep $c_{\mathrm{opt}}$ itself inside the feasible band and
loosen it by the same relative amount $\varepsilon$. Here $c_{\mathrm{opt}}$
is the \emph{optimised LP objective}, \texttt{n.objective} --- not a
re-derived $\text{capex}+\text{opex}$ statistic. For a \textbf{stochastic}
network (PyPSA's multi-scenario mode) it is automatically the
probability-weighted \emph{expected} cost $\sum_s p_s\, c_s^\top x_s$
already, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget} defines the near-optimal space
consistently for demand, price, \emph{and} stochastic runs without any
separate scenario-aware bookkeeping.

\subsection{Projection onto technology-capacity dimensions}
Exploring the full-dimensional $X_\varepsilon$ is neither tractable nor
useful for interpretation. We project onto a handful of \emph{technology
capacity} coordinates $y = \sigma(x)$ --- each $y_i$ is the summed installed
capacity of one technology (the $\sigma$-aggregation map of Grochowicz et
al.\ \S 2.2). Exploring a single direction $d$ in this reduced space means
re-solving the original model once with the ordinary cost objective
replaced by $\pm\, d^\top \sigma(x)$, subject to the budget of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget} --- this is exactly \emph{Modelling to Generate
Alternatives}.

\subsection{Three tiers}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item[Tier 1 --- ranges.] For each dimension, minimise and maximise it
    alone under the budget: the per-technology min/max installed capacity.
    $\min > 0 \Rightarrow$ \emph{must-have}; $\max \approx 0 \Rightarrow$
    \emph{must-avoid}.
  \item[Tier 2 --- hull.] Sample many search directions $d$ in the selected
    capacity space, solve each, and take the convex hull of the resulting
    extreme points --- the approximated near-optimal polytope.
  \item[Tier 3 --- robustness.] Solve the cost-optimum and explore the hull
    independently for several weather/price years, intersect the resulting
    per-year polytopes, and take the \textbf{Chebyshev centre} (deepest
    interior point) of the intersection as the design that stays
    near-optimal \emph{simultaneously} across all years (Grochowicz et al.\
    \S 2.3--2.4).
\end{description}

\subsection{Shared cost bound and the Chebyshev centre}
For Tier 3, Grochowicz et al.\ (eq.\ 9) note that intersecting per-year
spaces that were each bounded by their \emph{own} optimum $c_{\mathrm{opt}}(i)$
is not directly comparable across years with different absolute cost
levels; a single shared bound
\begin{equation}
  c^\star = \max_i\, c_{\mathrm{opt}}(i)
\end{equation}
can instead be applied to every year's budget, so all years are relaxed
relative to the \emph{same} anchor. GreenBubble exposes this as
\texttt{robustness.cost\_bound: max} (default) versus \texttt{per\_year}.

Given $m$ per-year polytopes (each a \texttt{scipy.spatial.ConvexHull}),
their facet inequalities $a_i^\top y \le b_i$ are stacked into one system;
the Chebyshev centre of the intersection is found via the linear program
\begin{equation}
  \max_{y,\, r}\; r
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad
  a_i^\top y + r\,\lVert a_i \rVert_2 \le b_i \quad \forall i, \qquad r \ge 0,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the centre of the largest ball that fits inside every half-space
simultaneously. $r \le 0$ means the per-year near-optimal spaces do not
overlap at all --- itself a meaningful finding (no design is simultaneously
near-optimal in all years at that slack).

\section{Implementation architecture}
% =====================================================================
The feature lives in \texttt{scripts/near\_optimal.py} (core algorithms,
$\sim$670 lines), \texttt{scripts/snakemake\_near\_optimal.py} (orchestration
wrapper), the \texttt{explore\_near\_optimal} Snakemake rule, and an
\texttt{mga:} block in \texttt{config.yaml}. It runs \emph{after} a network
is solved --- it consumes the \texttt{*\_OPT.nc} network and never alters
the ordinary solve.

\subsection{Dimension auto-derivation}
A dimension is a technology; its value is the summed installed capacity
(MW) of every \emph{extendable}, \emph{costed} ($\texttt{capital\_cost}>0$)
component belonging to it. The set of selectable dimensions is
\textbf{auto-derived} from the built network --- nothing is hardcoded:
flipping a technology to \texttt{expansion: true} in GreenBubble's own
config automatically makes it available. The grouping key per component is
GreenBubble's own \texttt{comp\_tech\_map} (component name $\to$ technology),
falling back to the PyPSA \texttt{carrier} attribute. The resulting
structure is exactly the nested \texttt{weights} dictionary PyPSA's own MGA
API expects:
\begin{lstlisting}
{tech_key: {"Generator": {"p_nom": {comp_name: 1.0, ...}},
            "Store":     {"e_nom": {comp_name: 1.0, ...}}, ...}}
\end{lstlisting}
Users then pick a small subset (typically 4--5) by name; an unknown name
raises an error listing what is actually available for that network.

\subsection{The constraint-correctness problem}
\label{sec:constraint-problem}
This is the central engineering issue we had to solve, and the reason the
per-direction solve is \emph{not} a direct call to PyPSA's built-in MGA
helpers. GreenBubble adds project-specific custom Linopy constraints to
every ordinary solve (most importantly a maximum
renewable-electricity-to-grid-sales constraint,
\texttt{add\_max\_RE\_sales\_constraint}) via an \texttt{extra\_functionality}
hook. PyPSA's native \texttt{n.optimize.optimize\_mga} /
\texttt{optimize\_mga\_in\_direction} build their \emph{own} model
internally and \textbf{never call \texttt{extra\_functionality}} --- so
using them unmodified would silently drop our custom constraints and compute
the near-optimal space against a \emph{looser}, incorrect feasible region
(higher volumes, wrong must-have/must-avoid flags).

We therefore reproduce PyPSA's own per-direction MGA procedure manually,
step for step, and insert our constraint re-application in the middle:

\begin{lstlisting}
def _solve_mga_in_direction(n, direction, dimensions, c_opt, slack, ...):
    """Constraint-correct replacement for
    n.optimize.optimize_mga_in_direction."""
    m = n.optimize.create_model()                    # <- PyPSA
    cost_objective = m.objective                      # captured before overwrite
    apply_custom_constraints(n, m, n_flags=n_flags,    # <- GreenBubble
                              re_alpha=re_alpha)
    _add_budget_constraint(m, cost_objective,          # <- GreenBubble (Eq. 3)
                            c_opt, slack)

    m.objective = -sum(
        float(direction[k]) *
        n.optimize.build_linexpr_from_weights(dimensions[k], model=m)  # <- PyPSA
        for k in dimensions if direction.get(k, 0)
    )

    status, condition = n.optimize.solve_model(...)   # <- PyPSA
    coords = n.optimize.project_solved(dimensions) if status == "ok" else None
                                                        # <- PyPSA
    return status, condition, coords
\end{lstlisting}
Every Tier-1, Tier-2, and Tier-3 solve funnels through this one function, so
the near-optimal space is always computed against \emph{exactly} the same
feasible region as an ordinary GreenBubble solve.

\subsection{Stochastic-network support}
GreenBubble also supports a PyPSA stochastic (multi-scenario) optimisation
mode, orthogonal to anything in either cited paper. Since the shared
first-stage investment variables ($p_{\mathrm{nom}}$, $e_{\mathrm{nom}}$)
are the same across scenarios, Tiers 1--2 work \emph{transparently} on a
stochastic network once the scenario-broadcast \texttt{(scenario, name)}
MultiIndex on the static component tables is collapsed back to plain
component names (\texttt{is\_stochastic}, \texttt{\_flat\_static}); the MGA
solve itself needs no change since \texttt{n.objective} is already the
expected cost. Tier 3 is deliberately \textbf{skipped} on a stochastic
network: its scenarios already span multiple years/conditions, so
intersecting \emph{again} across years would double-count the same
uncertainty axis.

% =====================================================================
\section{What we reused versus what we wrote from scratch}
\label{sec:provenance}
% =====================================================================
This is the section most relevant to our discussion. We deliberately built
as much as possible on top of \textbf{PyPSA's own native MGA machinery}
(which is Fabian Neumann's reference implementation of Neumann \& Brown
2021, shipped inside PyPSA itself) rather than re-deriving low-level LP
mechanics. \textbf{We did not have access to a Grochowicz-authored code
repository for the 2023 paper} --- our reference for the robustness
extension is the published paper's equations and section numbers only.
Below is an honest, function-level account of provenance.

\subsection{Reused directly via PyPSA's public API (not copied, called)}
\begingroup
\newcommand{\fnbreak}[1]{\allowbreak\texttt{#1}}
\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.7cm}p{2.6cm}p{7.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{PyPSA function} & \textbf{Module} & \textbf{Used for} \\
\midrule
\texttt{n}\fnbreak{.optimize}\fnbreak{.create\_model()} & \texttt{pypsa}\fnbreak{.optimization} &
  Building the Linopy LP model for each MGA solve \\
\texttt{n}\fnbreak{.optimize}\fnbreak{.build\_linexpr\_}\allowbreak\texttt{from\_weights()} & \texttt{pypsa}\fnbreak{.optimization} &
  Turning a $\sigma$-weights dict into a Linopy linear expression --- used
  for both the direction objective and Tier 3's ``fix aggregate capacity''
  constraint \\
\texttt{n}\fnbreak{.optimize}\fnbreak{.solve\_model()} & \texttt{pypsa}\fnbreak{.optimization} &
  Solving each direction's LP with the chosen solver/profile \\
\texttt{n}\fnbreak{.optimize}\fnbreak{.project\_solved()} & \texttt{pypsa}\fnbreak{.optimization} &
  Reading solved capacities back, projected onto the selected dimensions \\
\texttt{generate\_directions\_}\allowbreak\texttt{halton}\fnbreak{/evenly\_spaced}\fnbreak{/random()} & \texttt{pypsa}\fnbreak{.optimization}\fnbreak{.mga} &
  Sampling search directions for Tier 2 (used unmodified via a thin
  wrapper) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
\endgroup

The \texttt{weights} dictionary schema itself (Section~\ref{sec:constraint-problem})
is also PyPSA's own MGA API convention, adopted as-is so our dimension
registry interoperates with these functions without translation.

\subsection{Implemented from scratch (GreenBubble-original)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The manual per-direction solve} (\texttt{\_solve\_mga\_in\_direction}),
    reproducing PyPSA's \texttt{optimize\_mga\_in\_direction} step-by-step
    specifically to insert \texttt{apply\_custom\_constraints} --- the fix
    described in Section~\ref{sec:constraint-problem}. This is the one
    piece of PyPSA-adjacent logic we \emph{did} re-derive, and only because
    the public API leaves no hook to extend it instead.
  \item \textbf{The sign-robust budget} (Eq.~\ref{eq:budget}) generalising
    Neumann \& Brown's $(1+\varepsilon)\,c_{\mathrm{opt}}$ to a
    price-driven, potentially negative objective.
  \item \textbf{Dimension auto-derivation} (\texttt{available\_dimensions},
    \texttt{resolve\_dimensions}) from GreenBubble's own technology config
    and component-to-technology mapping --- no equivalent exists in PyPSA
    or in the cited papers, which assume dimensions are given.
  \item \textbf{Stochastic-network transparency} (Section 3.3) --- an
    extension neither paper addresses, since PyPSA's scenario API postdates
    both.
  \item \textbf{Tier 3 in full}: the shared-bound anchor $c^\star$ (eq.\ 9),
    and the Chebyshev-centre LP itself
    (\texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog} over the stacked hull facet
    inequalities) --- implemented from scratch in Python/NumPy/SciPy
    directly off the paper's \S 2.3--2.4 equations, since no reference code
    was available to adapt.
  \item \textbf{Everything operational around the algorithm}: the
    Snakemake rule and wrapper, the three-tier \texttt{mga:} config block,
    CSV/JSON output schema, and all plotting
    (\texttt{plot\_ranges}, \texttt{plot\_hull\_projections},
    \texttt{plot\_robustness} --- see Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verification against the reference implementation}
\label{sec:verification}
While preparing this note we located the official code release for the 2023
paper: \url{https://github.com/aleks-g/intersecting-near-opt-spaces}
(tag \texttt{v1.0.1}, GPL-3.0-or-later, Koen van Greevenbroek \& Aleksander
Grochowicz). We compared our implementation against it directly,
function by function:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Chebyshev centre --- confirmed identical.} Their
    \texttt{geometry.ch\_centre()} solves precisely the same LP as our
    \texttt{chebyshev\_centre()}: \mbox{$\max r \text{ s.t. }
    a_i^\top x + r\lVert a_i\rVert \le b_i$} over a hull's
    \texttt{ConvexHull.equations}, same sign handling of the qhull
    convention. We arrived at this independently from the paper's \S 2.4,
    and it matches their reference code exactly. Their version solves the
    LP with Gurobi (\texttt{gurobipy}) directly; ours uses
    \texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog}, a deliberate solver-agnostic choice
    consistent with GreenBubble's documented no-Gurobi-licence fallback path
    (Section~\ref{sec:results}, validation notes) --- same formulation,
    different backend.
  \item \textbf{Shared robustness bound --- confirmed identical.} Their
    \texttt{calc\_obj\_bound.py} computes exactly
    \texttt{obj\_bound = (1 + eps) * max(objs)}, i.e.\ our
    $c^\star = \max_i c_{\mathrm{opt}}(i)$ (eq.\ 9), letter for letter (up
    to our sign-robust $|c_{\mathrm{opt}}|$ generalisation, which their code
    has no analogue for --- their objective is always a positive investment
    cost, never GreenBubble's negative price-mode net revenue).
  \item \textbf{Per-direction MGA solve --- same algorithm, necessarily
    different API.} Their \texttt{utilities}\allowbreak\texttt{.solve\_network\_in\_direction}
    does the same thing ours does (near-optimal budget as an
    \texttt{extra\_functionality} constraint, custom direction objective,
    solve) but against \texttt{pypsa.linopf}/\texttt{linopt} --- PyPSA's
    pre-Linopy optimisation API. GreenBubble runs on PyPSA 1.0.7's Linopy-based
    \texttt{n.optimize} API, which that code cannot run against unmodified;
    our \texttt{\_solve\_mga\_in\_direction} is the same algorithm ported to
    the current API, not a literal reuse.
  \item \textbf{Tier 2 (hull) --- a real, disclosed difference.} Their
    \texttt{compute\_near\_opt.py} is substantially more sophisticated than
    ours: an \emph{adaptive} direction sampler (facet normals of the
    current hull, directions touching the current Chebyshev ball, or both
    combined) with volume- or centre-shift convergence criteria, run until
    convergence or a direction budget is exhausted --- versus our simpler
    fixed-count Halton/random/evenly-spaced sampling via PyPSA's built-in
    generators. Theirs converges to a tighter hull for the same solve
    budget; ours is simpler to reason about and bounded by
    \texttt{n\_directions}. See Section~\ref{sec:dev3} --- closing this gap
    is exactly what we are doing next.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reuse landed: \texttt{near-optimal\_dev3}}
\label{sec:dev3}
Their \texttt{geometry.py} module (Chebyshev centre, hull intersection,
facet-normal/hypersphere-based direction sampling) is pure
NumPy/SciPy/(Gurobi) geometry with \textbf{no dependency on the PyPSA
optimisation API} --- unlike the per-direction solve, it does not need
porting to work with GreenBubble's PyPSA version, only the LP backend needs
adapting (Gurobi $\to$ solver-agnostic, as we already do for the Chebyshev
centre). This made it the practical, low-risk target for direct code
reuse, and it is now fully wired in and validated on a local
\texttt{near-optimal\_dev3} branch (not yet pushed --- see the licence
discussion in Section~\ref{sec:honest-summary}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Their geometry utilities (\texttt{ch\_centre}, \texttt{intersection},
    \texttt{facet\_normals}, the hypersphere samplers, and the three
    facet/Chebyshev-ball direction generators originally split across
    \texttt{geometry.py} and \texttt{compute\_near\_opt.py}) are vendored
    verbatim (module structure, docstrings, algorithm) under their original
    GPL-3.0-or-later licence and attribution, in a module clearly separated
    from GreenBubble's MIT-licensed code (GPL licensing does not permit
    merging the two) --- the only change is porting the Gurobi LP calls to
    \texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog}.
  \item \texttt{chebyshev\_centre()} now calls their ported function
    directly rather than a separate from-scratch LP; a new
    \texttt{intersect\_hulls()} exposes their qhull-based
    \texttt{HalfspaceIntersection} approach for the full intersected
    polytope, not just its centre.
  \item A new \texttt{explore\_hull\_adaptive()} runs their iterative
    facet-/Chebyshev-ball-based direction sampler in place of our fixed-count
    Halton sampling for Tier 2, using their exact convergence criteria
    (volume- or centre-shift, \texttt{conv\_eps}/\texttt{conv\_iter}) ---
    only the per-direction \emph{solve} is GreenBubble's own
    constraint-correct one, since their code solves via the pre-Linopy
    \texttt{pypsa.linopf} API in parallel worker processes.
  \item \textbf{Verified twice}: first standalone (10 seed + 5 adaptive
    solves, $\sim$3 minutes with \texttt{gurobi-}\allowbreak\texttt{barrier-fast};
    the identical run with \texttt{gurobi-default} did not finish a single
    direction in 36+ minutes and had to be killed --- an independent, real-network
    confirmation of the solver-degeneracy finding in
    Section~\ref{sec:results}), then again through the real Snakemake
    pipeline described next (22 points, 12 adaptive iterations, converged).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Staged pipeline: mirroring their rule decomposition}
GreenBubble's default \texttt{explore\_near\_optimal} rule runs all three
tiers inline in one Snakemake rule/script. Their reference pipeline instead
splits into discrete rules --- \texttt{compute\_optimum} $\to$ \texttt{mga}
(cardinal seed) $\to$ \texttt{compute\_near\_opt} (adaptive refinement)
$\to$ \texttt{intersect\_near\_optimal} $\to$ \texttt{compute\_robust\_*}
--- which tracks the paper's own stepwise structure closely. We mirrored
that decomposition (not the repo itself) in a new
\texttt{rules/near\_optimal\_staged.smk}, reachable only by explicit target
name (not wired into the default \texttt{rule all}):

\begin{center}
\small
\texttt{nos\_seed} $\to$ \texttt{nos\_hull\_adaptive}\\[4pt]
\texttt{nos\_year\_optimum}\{2023,2024,2025\} $\to$ \texttt{nos\_cost\_bound}
$\to$ \texttt{nos\_year\_hull}\{2023,2024,2025\} $\to$ \texttt{nos\_intersect}
$\to$ \texttt{nos\_robust\_exact}
\end{center}

Each stage writes an intermediate file, so e.g.\ re-running the intersection
after a tuning change no longer requires re-solving three years of networks
--- exactly the caching benefit their decomposition buys. \texttt{nos\_seed}
solves the $2k$ cardinal directions once and derives \emph{both} the Tier-1
ranges table \emph{and} \texttt{explore\_hull\_adaptive}'s real seed points
from the same solves (no duplicate solving, and no seed points
reconstructed/approximated from the ranges table, which only keeps the
projected scalar per dimension). \texttt{nos\_robust\_exact} realises the
Chebyshev centre against a single reference network via
\texttt{realise\_design} (Grochowicz et al.'s ``exact'' strategy); their
three heuristic allocation strategies (\texttt{conservative}/\texttt{mean}/
\texttt{naive}) and simultaneous multi-year feasibility check are not
ported yet.

\textbf{Real end-to-end run} (\texttt{snakemake nos\_hull\_adaptive},
tutorial 5's exact config, all 5 pipeline steps completing): 10 real
cardinal seed points $+$ 12 adaptive iterations $=$ 22 points, converged on
the volume criterion, $c_{\mathrm{opt}} = 66.6\,\text{M}$ (consistent with
the dev2 baseline's $\sim$67\,M --- confirms it's the same problem, not a
config drift), Chebyshev radius of this single hull $= 3.15$.

Re-running the 3-year robustness comparison (Section~\ref{sec:results}'s
network, dev2's fixed 20-direction Halton sampler vs.\ dev3's adaptive

apples-to-apples reading of what the adaptive sampler buys:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
dev2 (Halton, fixed count) & 0.58 & 147.6 & 5.2 & 53.2 & 0.85 \\
dev3 (adaptive)            & 2.08 & 132.7 & 17.4 & 52.5 & 3.45 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

($c_{\mathrm{opt}}$ per year matched to within rounding across both runs:

sampler found a \textbf{3.6$\times$ larger} Chebyshev radius for a
comparable solve budget --- it characterises the intersection region more
completely than a fixed direction count, and lands on a noticeably
different (more solar/methanolisation/battery-leaning) centre as a result.

\subsection{Capacity- vs.\ investment-weighted dimensions}
\label{sec:weighting}
While comparing implementations we noticed their actual \texttt{projection:}
config (\texttt{config/config-}\allowbreak\texttt{default.yaml}) weights
\emph{every} dimension by \texttt{capital\_cost}:
\begin{lstlisting}
projection:
  onwind:
    - {c: "Generator", v: "p_nom", carrier: "onwind",
       weight: "capital_cost", scale_by_years: True}
\end{lstlisting}
i.e.\ their dimensions are \textbf{annualised investment cost} (EUR/year)
per technology, not installed capacity --- whereas GreenBubble's
\texttt{available\_dimensions} (Section~\ref{sec:constraint-problem})
hardcodes weight $=1$ per component, i.e.\ pure \textbf{MW}. Same PyPSA
mechanism (\texttt{build\_linexpr\_from\_weights} just computes
$\sum w_i \cdot \text{var}_i$ for whatever $w$ it's given), different
modelling choice layered on top --- the near-optimal-space maths itself is
agnostic to what a ``dimension'' physically represents.

We added \texttt{mga.dimension\_weight: capacity \textbar\ investment} so
both are selectable (default \texttt{capacity}, unchanged behaviour).
Verified exactly on a real network: the ratio of a component's
investment-weighted to capacity-weighted projected value equals its
\texttt{capital\_cost} to full float precision (onwind:
$128{,}306.330322\ldots$ both ways), and the full staged pipeline
(\texttt{nos\_seed} $\to$ \texttt{nos\_hull\_adaptive}) runs correctly
end-to-end in \texttt{investment} mode too: 5/5 Snakemake steps, 17 points
(10 seed $+$ 7 adaptive), converged. \textbf{Open question for the
meeting}: was cost-weighting a deliberate methodological choice for your
study (e.g.\ to put technologies with very different EUR/MW on a common
economic axis), or would capacity weighting have worked equally well for
your results?

\subsection{The honest summary}
\label{sec:honest-summary}
We reused PyPSA's low-level MGA \emph{mechanics} as much as the public API
allowed, and reimplemented the one piece it doesn't expose a hook for
(constraint-correct solving). Grochowicz et al.\ (2023)'s specific
contribution --- multi-year robustness via intersection and the Chebyshev
centre --- was implemented from the paper's mathematics alone in the current
released version, and Section~\ref{sec:verification} confirms it is
mathematically identical to their reference code where the two are directly
comparable; Section~\ref{sec:dev3} now reuses their actual code (Tier 2's
adaptive sampler, Tier 3's intersection) directly, license boundary
permitting. \textbf{We would value your read on whether
\texttt{near-optimal\_dev3}'s reuse is the right scope}, and on the
solver-degeneracy behaviour reported there --- has the
\texttt{gurobi-default}/\texttt{gurobi-barrier-fast} gap come up in your own
runs of this algorithm?

\section{The decisive comparison: 7-year robust NOS design vs.\ equal-weight stochastic optimum}
\label{sec:decisive}
% =====================================================================
Tutorial 5 (Section~\ref{sec:results}) demonstrates the machinery on a
3-year slice with a small, illustrative dimension set. The run described
here is the one we actually care about for the project: a full,
production-scale comparison between \textbf{two structurally different ways
of producing a single design that is good across several weather/price
years}, run side by side on the identical underlying network, demand
targets, and years, so that any difference in the resulting capacity mix is
attributable to the \emph{method}, not to a config mismatch.

\subsection{Two philosophies for one design}
\label{sec:decisive-philosophy}
Both sides of this comparison answer the question ``what capacity mix
should we build if we don't want to bet on one specific weather/price
year?'' --- but they answer it with different mathematics, and the
difference is exactly the contrast Grochowicz et al.\ (2023) motivate their
paper with (\S 1):

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item[Equal-weight stochastic optimisation.] PyPSA's native
    multi-scenario mode solves \emph{one} LP with a shared first-stage
    investment ($p_{\mathrm{nom}}$, $e_{\mathrm{nom}}$) and per-scenario
    second-stage dispatch, minimising the \textbf{probability-weighted
    expected cost} $\sum_s p_s\, c_s^\top x_s$. With seven years weighted
    equally ($p_s = 1/7$), this is the classic two-stage stochastic-programming
    answer: the design that is cheapest \emph{on average} over the seven
    years, with no guarantee that it is good in any \emph{particular} one of
    them --- a design can be the expected-cost optimum while still being
    badly over budget in the single worst year, as long as it is
    compensated by being especially cheap in others.
  \item[Chebyshev-centre robust NOS design.] The Tier-3 procedure of
    Section~\ref{sec:results} instead solves the cost-optimum
    \emph{independently} for each of the seven years, builds each year's own
    $\varepsilon$-near-optimal hull (Eq.~\ref{eq:budget}), intersects all
    seven hulls, and takes the Chebyshev centre of the intersection. This
    design carries \textbf{no probabilities at all} --- it asks only
    whether a capacity mix exists that stays within $\varepsilon$ of
    optimal \emph{simultaneously in every one of the seven years}, and if
    so, picks the one with the largest margin ($r$, the Chebyshev radius) to
    every year's boundary. It is a \emph{feasibility-of-robustness}
    statement, not an expectation: $r > 0$ is itself the finding that a
    single design can satisfy all seven years' near-optimality at once;
    $r \le 0$ would mean no design does, at this slack.
\end{description}
Neither is more ``correct'' than the other; they trade off different
things. Stochastic optimisation needs no slack parameter but needs
probabilities (here, an equal-weight assumption standing in for genuine
year-likelihood estimates) and only ever reports an \emph{average}; the
robust NOS design needs no probabilities but needs a slack $\varepsilon$
and reports a design that is \emph{simultaneously} acceptable everywhere it
applies, at the cost of usually costing a little more in the design's own
reference year than that year's true optimum would. Running both against
the identical seven years, targets, and network is what lets us actually
quantify that trade-off for GreenBubble's own technology mix, rather than
taking it on faith from the literature.

\subsection{Run configuration}
Both runs share: \texttt{targets.driver: demand} with
$\texttt{demand\_H2} = 200{,}000$, $\texttt{demand\_CH4} = 300{,}000$,
$\texttt{demand\_meoh} = 15{,}000$ (MWh/y, flat profiles),
\texttt{clustering.temporal.resolution: 3h}, and the seven weather/price
years 2019--2025 each at \texttt{CO2\_cost = CO2\_cost\_ref\_year = 100}
(no inter-year cost escalation --- isolating weather/price variability as
the only thing that differs year to year). \texttt{n\_flags} enable
biogas, central heat, renewables, electrolysis, methanolisation, and
storage; \texttt{methanation} is off for this comparison (see
Section~\ref{sec:decisive-notes}).

\textbf{NOS side} (\texttt{run\_name: stochastic\_vs\_NOS\_D}, this document):
run through \texttt{rules/near\_optimal\_staged.smk}'s
\texttt{nos\_robust\_validate} target (Section~\ref{sec:dev3}) --- i.e.\ the
\emph{adaptive, Grochowicz-method} sampler, not the fixed-count Halton
sampler of \texttt{explore\_near\_optimal}, with dimensions
\texttt{[onwind, solar, AEC, methanolisation, battery]} and
$\varepsilon = \texttt{slack} = 0.05$. Concretely this executes seven
independent \texttt{nos\_year\_optimum} solves, seven
\texttt{nos\_year\_hull} adaptive Tier-2 explorations under the shared bound
$c^\star = \max_i c_{\mathrm{opt}}(i)$, \texttt{nos\_intersect}'s Chebyshev
centre of all seven hulls, \texttt{nos\_robust\_exact}'s realisation of that
centre as a full network design, and finally
\texttt{nos\_robust\_validate}'s simultaneous multi-year feasibility check
--- the realised design's capacities fixed non-extendable and re-dispatched
against each year's own real cost-optimal network, with a load-shedding
safety valve turning any shortfall into a graded curtailment number instead
of an opaque solver failure (Section~\ref{sec:dev3}, \emph{"Reuse landed"}).

\textbf{Stochastic side}: the same seven years as an equal-weight
(\,$p_s = 1/7$\,) PyPSA multi-scenario network, single joint solve.

\subsection{Solver choice and a numerical-stability finding along the way}
\label{sec:decisive-notes}
Getting to a trustworthy run required resolving two real bugs uncovered
while preparing this comparison, both worth recording since they bear
directly on how much to trust any single solve's feasibility/infeasibility
verdict on a network this size ($\sim$625k rows, $\sim$250k columns at 3h
resolution):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{A units mismatch on the biogas plant's expansion cap.}
    \texttt{n\_config}'s \texttt{max capacity} for the digester link is
    documented (and used elsewhere, e.g.\ its \texttt{initial capacity}
    conversion) as MW of \emph{CH4 output}, but \texttt{p\_nom\_max} was
    being set directly from that value onto a link whose \texttt{p\_nom}
    bounds \emph{digester-input} flow (t/h dry matter) --- silently
    capping real deliverable CH4 at roughly half the intended figure
    (efficiency $\approx 0.477$). Fixed by applying the same
    digester-efficiency division already used for \texttt{initial capacity}.
  \item \textbf{Solver-algorithm disagreement on a degenerate cost-optimal
    solve.} With \texttt{methanation} enabled, the base year-optimal solve
    returned \emph{three different verdicts} depending on the Gurobi
    algorithm: plain dual simplex (\texttt{DualReductions=0}) reported
    \emph{unbounded}; the numerically robust homogeneous-barrier method
    (\texttt{NumericFocus=3, BarHomogeneous=1}) reported \emph{infeasible};
    and Gurobi's own internal re-solve during \texttt{computeIIS()} reported
    \emph{feasible}. The objective's coefficient range spans nine orders of
    magnitude ($5\times10^{-6}$ to $4\times10^{5}$), consistent with the kind
    of ill-conditioning that produces exactly this disagreement. Disabling
    \texttt{methanation} alone (leaving everything else, including a still
    free-priced digester feedstock, unchanged) resolved it to a clean
    \texttt{optimal} --- narrowing the remaining issue to the biological
    methanation link's economics, not yet fully root-caused as of this
    writing.
\end{itemize}
Given this, and consistent with the solver-degeneracy finding already
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} and Section~\ref{sec:dev3}
(\texttt{gurobi-default} unusably slow on this class of degenerate MGA
objective; \texttt{highs-fast} an outright correctness risk), this run uses
\texttt{gurobi-numeric-focus} (\texttt{NumericFocus: 3, Method: 2,
Crossover: 0, BarHomogeneous: 1}) rather than the faster
\texttt{gurobi-barrier-fast} used elsewhere in this document --- the same
speed class (still \texttt{Crossover: 0}), but the one setting combination
that gave an unambiguous answer on this specific network during debugging.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:decisive-results}

\subsubsection{First attempts: an empty intersection, and why}
The first two full seven-year Tier-3 runs both came back with \emph{no}
robust design: identical setup (dimensions, years, slack \emph{or}
7\,\% slack, and after the ramp-limit fix of
Section~\ref{sec:decisive-notes} identical ramp-limit-free technical
assumptions), \texttt{nos\_intersect} reported
\texttt{feasible\_intersection: false}, \texttt{chebyshev\_radius: NaN} in
both cases, and \texttt{nos\_robust\_exact} correctly refused to realise a
design rather than return something misleading. The per-year cost-optimal
objectives explained why: \textbf{2022 was roughly half the cost-optimal
objective of every other year} (28.09\,M€/y vs.\ 53--61\,M€/y elsewhere),
and its optimal capacity mix sat in a completely different region of the
5-dimensional space --- 159.6\,MW solar and 125.1\,MW battery, versus
$\le$1.4\,MW and $\le$6.7\,MW respectively in every other year.
Digging into the revenue structure (\texttt{El3\_to\_DK1}, the
RE-to-grid export link, priced at each snapshot's real spot price) showed
this was RE-export arbitrage: 2022's extreme spot-price spikes (the
European energy-price crisis year) made 55.94\,M€/y of export revenue
available at the default \texttt{max\_RE\_to\_grid=0.3} cap --- \emph{almost
twice 2022's entire net objective} --- versus 11.23\,M€/y for 2024 and
essentially none for 2019. 2022 wasn't cheaper because its underlying
system was cheaper to build; it was cheaper because the optimal design
was aggressively sized to arbitrage a price spike unique to that year.

\subsubsection{Tightening the export cap, and the successful run}
Sweeping \texttt{max\_RE\_to\_grid} down (cheap check: \texttt{nos\_year\_optimum}
only, all seven years, no hull) tracked 2022 converging back toward the
other six as the arbitrage channel narrowed:

The matched equal-weight (\,$p_s=1/7$\,) stochastic solve at the same

\subsubsection{The cost of robustness, head to head}
With both sides now run under identical technical assumptions ($\alpha =
0.05$, ramp-limit-free, same seven years, same targets), the comparison
Section~\ref{sec:decisive-philosophy} set out to make is now a real number:
the robust Chebyshev-centre design costs \textbf{65.39\,M€/y}, the
stochastic expected-cost optimum costs \textbf{63.98\,M€/y} --- a gap of
\textbf{1.41\,M€/y ($\approx$\,2.2\,\%)}. That premium is what
``no probabilities, but simultaneous $\varepsilon$-near-optimality in
\emph{every} year'' costs over ``cheapest on average, no guarantee in any
one year'' for this network, at this slack. It is a strikingly small
premium for a materially stronger guarantee: the robust design isn't just
cheap on average, it is verified (Section 8.4.2) to need essentially zero
load-shedding in \emph{any} of the seven years, including the one
(2022) that initially broke the na\"ive version of this same analysis.

\emph{Note on comparability}: the technology-data cost tables were
refreshed by the pipeline's own \texttt{onstart} staleness check between
the diagnostic $\alpha$-sweep above and this final run, so the earlier
$\alpha=0.3$ stochastic result reported in an earlier draft of this
document (58.16\,M€/y) used a slightly different cost vintage than the
numbers here; both sides of the final head-to-head comparison in this
subsection used the identical (refreshed) cost table.

\label{sec:decisive-grid}
Not one of the five MGA dimensions, but a natural result to read off both
solved networks --- the external grid connection's sized capacity,
import (\texttt{DK1\_to\_El\_*}, one link per on-site agent, summed) and

\begin{center}
\small

The stochastic design leans harder on export (27.9 vs.\ 19.1\,MW) despite

figures is bumping against an artificial ceiling --- the sizing is purely
economic.

\textbf{An open modelling question worth flagging}: import and export are
built as \emph{separate} extendable links (one \texttt{DK1\_to\_El\_*} per
agent, plus the standalone \texttt{El3\_to\_DK1}), and --- checked directly
against both solved networks --- \emph{every one of them carries the
identical} \texttt{electricity grid connection} \textbf{capital-cost rate}
(17,767.89\,€/MW/y), charged independently on each link's own
\texttt{p\_nom\_opt}. That is, the model pays capital cost on
\emph{import capacity + export capacity} added together, not on
$\max(\text{import}, \text{export})$ as a single shared bidirectional
connection would suggest. If GreenLab's real grid connection is one
physical asset usable in both directions, this over-counts its cost by
exactly the smaller direction's own contribution --- 339,079\,€/y for the
Chebyshev centre design, 495,192\,€/y for the stochastic design (both
happen to be the export link's own annualised capex, since export is the
smaller of the two capacities in both designs). We could not determine
from the code alone whether this is intentional (e.g.\ representing
genuinely separate import/export equipment) or an oversight --- worth
resolving before treating either design's absolute cost as final.

  \item Any feedback on the solver-degeneracy finding in

  \item Section~\ref{sec:decisive} ran the robust Chebyshev-centre design

    \emph{which} year(s) are driving an empty intersection short of
    dropping them one at a time and re-running?
\end{enumerate}
